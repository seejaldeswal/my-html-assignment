Q1
Remembering · Knowledge

List four primary characteristics of a modern digital computer and identify the key hardware technology in each of the five generations.

Four primary characteristics

  1. Speed: performs millions or billions of operations per second.
  2. Accuracy: produces correct results when the data and instructions are correct.
  3. Storage: stores large amounts of data and retrieves it quickly.
  4. Automation and diligence: follows instructions automatically and can repeat tasks without getting tired.

Five generations of computers

GenerationMain technologyKey features / examples
First (approx. 1940s–1950s)Vacuum tubesVery large, hot, expensive; machine language. Example: ENIAC.
Second (1950s–1960s)TransistorsSmaller, faster, more reliable and energy-efficient.
Third (1960s–1970s)Integrated circuits (ICs)Many components on one chip; operating systems became more common.
Fourth (1970s onward)Microprocessors using VLSIPersonal computers, compact devices, affordable computing.
Fifth (current / developing)ULSI, parallel processing and AI-oriented technologiesAI applications, natural-language systems, robotics and advanced processing.
Conclusion: computer generations are mainly distinguished by the hardware technology used to build their processing circuits.

Explain the flow of data, instructions and control signals within a computer using a block diagram.

A computer system consists of input, memory, the CPU and output units. Data and instructions enter through input devices; the CPU processes them using the control unit and ALU; results may be stored in memory and sent to output devices.

Role of each unit

  • Input unit: accepts data and instructions and converts them into machine-readable form.
  • Memory: holds instructions, input data, intermediate results and final results.
  • Control Unit: fetches and decodes instructions, then sends control signals to coordinate operations.
  • ALU: performs arithmetic operations and logical comparisons.
  • Registers: provide very fast temporary storage inside the CPU.
  • Output unit: presents results as text, images, sound or printed output.

Data buses carry data, address buses identify memory locations, and control buses carry signals such as read and write.

Why tablets suit CAD and art

  • Stylus input allows natural drawing and tracing.
  • Pressure sensitivity can vary line thickness for illustration.
  • Accurate coordinate input supports tracing and detailed geometry.
  • For CAD, a mouse is still common for menus and 3D navigation; a tablet is useful for sketching and annotation.
Selection: choose a mouse for affordable everyday use, a trackball where desk space is limited, and a graphics tablet when pen precision and natural strokes matter.
Q6
Evaluating · Evaluation

Assess a drum/flatbed plotter versus an ultra-high-definition monitor and recommend a suitable system for civil-engineering blueprints.

FactorDrum / flatbed plotterUltra-high-definition monitor
OutputPermanent physical drawing on paper/mediaDigital image on screen
Scale and detailCan print large-format, dimensionally detailed plansExcellent zoom and on-screen detail, but limited by screen size
SharingPhysical copies can be distributedEasy electronic sharing and collaboration
Cost considerationsEquipment, paper, ink/toner and maintenanceDisplay cost, graphics hardware and power
Best roleSubmission, site use, signing and archival printReview, CAD editing, zooming and checking layers

Recommendation

For civil-engineering blueprints that must be submitted, measured, or used on-site, select a large-format plotter as the primary output device. Use an UHD monitor as a complementary device for editing and inspection.

  • Resolution: choose a plotter resolution and line quality appropriate for fine CAD lines and text.
  • Scalability: confirm the required paper width (for example, A1/A0) and supported media.
  • Long-term costs: estimate consumables, maintenance and expected print volume.
  • Reliability: check service availability and driver compatibility with the CAD software.
A plotter is best when a physical, large-scale drawing is required; a monitor is best for interactive review. A combined setup offers the strongest workflow.
Q7
Evaluating · Evaluation

Critique the reliability and security vulnerabilities of vision-based iris/face recognition compared with voice recognition in high-ambient-noise, public settings.

IssueFace / iris recognitionVoice recognition
Environmental effectFace: lighting, camera angle, masks and image quality matter. Iris: camera distance, eye visibility and capture quality matter.Strongly affected by background noise, echoes, distance and microphone quality.
Spoofing riskPhotos, video or artificial eyes may attack weak systems; liveness detection helps.Recordings, replay, voice imitation or synthetic speech may attack weak systems.
PrivacyBiometric images/templates are sensitive and difficult to replace if exposed.Voice samples can reveal identity and other personal information.
AccessibilityMay not work equally well for every person or physical condition.Can be difficult for people with speech differences or in noisy environments.
MitigationsLiveness checks, secure templates, controlled camera position, human fallback.Noise-cancelling microphones, anti-replay checks, challenge phrases, human fallback.

Operational recommendation

  • In high ambient noise, voice recognition alone is a poor choice because the signal may be unclear.
  • Face recognition can be more practical where cameras and lighting are controlled; iris systems can be accurate with good capture equipment and user cooperation.
  • No biometric is perfect. Use multi-factor authentication (for example, card plus biometric), rate limits, encryption, access controls and audit logs.
  • Provide an accessible non-biometric alternative, clear consent and a policy for deletion/retention of biometric data.
Conclusion: choose based on the threat model and environment, test false-acceptance and false-rejection rates on the actual user population, and never treat a biometric match as completely risk-free.
